Hey, I'm a PhD student in the Department of Physics & Astronomy at University College London, working on extragalactic astrophysics with the James Webb Space Telescope, supervised by Dr. Guido Roberts-Borsani.

The telescope has opened up the first billion years of cosmic history to detailed study for the first time, and what it found there has not been what we expected. Galaxies from that era look brighter and more massive than they have any right to be so early, and their chemistry has been just as surprising.

Many of them contain far more nitrogen, relative to oxygen, than the galaxies we see around us today. That matters because different stars release different elements at different times, some within a few million years, others over hundreds of millions. The mix of elements in a galaxy is therefore a record of what it has already done, and finding this much nitrogen so soon after the Big Bang is hard to explain.

Why it happens is not understood, and that is what I work on. The open question is whether this is a quirk of a handful of unusually bright galaxies, or something the early population does more generally.

Before UCL I read Natural Sciences at Durham and stayed on for an MSc in Data Science, then did an MPhil in Data Intensive Science at Cambridge.

Away from the desk I am usually somewhere between a hockey pitch, a golf course and a tennis court, playing hockey for Richmond Hockey Club. I am also building Bluebell AI, a startup working to level the playing field in education. bluebellai.org
