Hey, I'm Raunaq Rai, an astrophysics PhD student at University College London, supervised by Dr. Guido Roberts-Borsani. I use the James Webb Space Telescope, the most powerful observatory ever built, to study the first galaxies that formed in the Universe.

Webb has opened up the first billion years of cosmic history to detailed study for the first time, and the galaxies it found there are not made of what we expected.

The Big Bang produced almost nothing but hydrogen and helium. Every other element — the carbon in your body, the oxygen you breathe, the nitrogen in the air — was built inside stars and scattered back out into space when those stars died. Different kinds of stars build different elements, and they take very different amounts of time to do it: the most massive live and die within a few million years, while smaller ones take hundreds of millions. A galaxy's chemistry is therefore a record of which stars it has made, and how long it has been making them.

That is what makes the early galaxies a puzzle. Many of them hold far more nitrogen, compared to oxygen, than the galaxies around us today — and so soon after the Big Bang, there has not been enough time for the usual sources of nitrogen to deliver it. Something about how these galaxies grew, and how they formed their stars, must have been different.

Why that happens is not understood, and it is what I work on. I ask whether the nitrogen excess is a quirk of a handful of unusual galaxies or the norm across the population, and constrain the mechanism(s) that cause it.

Before UCL I read Natural Sciences at Durham and stayed on for an MSc in Data Science, then did an MPhil in Data Intensive Science at Cambridge.
